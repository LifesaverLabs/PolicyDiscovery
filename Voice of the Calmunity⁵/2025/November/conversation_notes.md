Flight with J. Overseas
---------------------------
I was on a Spirit Airlines flight with an incredibly interesting, highly technical overseas American I'll call J. who was traveling to Ohio for family Thanksgiving. He works as a captain for very large yachts. He qualifies as among the brightest and best prepared people I've met.

The conversations was fascinating. But let me just get to the relevant takeaways with significant policy implications. While talking with him, and while remembering my own experiences living in and voting from China and my experience of voting as a Portuguese citizen abroad in Parliamentary Portuguese elections, I realized there might be a representation gap for overseas American citizens abroad. The American diaspora is huge — larger than Puerto Rico. No one knows exactly how large the American diaspora is but [estimates from the State Department estimates it at 7.6 million](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/americans-abroad-disillusioned-diaspora), roughly 2.4% of the United States population, and a population that would be larger than Arizona and all lesser populated states as the 14th most populated state, if the diaspora were a free-standing state of its own.

Currently, these diaspora citizens have to affiliate with the state of their last residence, and I found an article claiming [that only 7.8% of them actually exercise their suffrage](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-10-10/us-overseas-voters-under-the-microscope-election-trump-harris/104449064?utm_source=chatgpt.com). I want citizens like J. to vote — they have a distinct perspective on the world that points to expertise and policies that I rarely encounter day-to-day among less itinerant Americans. I innately feel that their voice and points-of-view are being drowned out by the lack of charismatic representatives focused on their needs and theories of change, and this isn't a small population — it's millions of Americans abroad who are dramatically less engaged than our domestic citizens but have valuable insights we don't have, like J.'s deep perspectives on everything from maritime safety⁵ and international law to his correcting me when I mistakenly suggested, carelessly, the question of whether Canada could ever, with enough time, join our United States.

Quick aside: What I really meant to say, and as J. suggested, was that Canada and the US become a new country together. I explained to J how I thought Mexico was an even harder lift; the level of economic development is very different and it would be very challenging getting Mexico up to per capita income levels similar to United States and Canada without a lot of handwaving or without a technological revolution facilitated by robots. There's just a lot of economic development that needs to happen. And without that, it gets difficult figuring out a fair almost-Pareto optimal social contract that keeps everyone happy and thriving. But inviting Canada and United States to merge into a new country is something that's arguably fairly pie-in-the-sky at this stage, until a group with Hippocratic harmless ethics gets enough influence to make such a proposal in a credible, mutually supportive way that could lead to an engagement and possibly a political marriage. 

Going back to the main question of the American diaspora, I've done some prelim research since on diaspora policy in Portugal, Italy, and France. All three countries guarantee representation to their diaspora, their citizens abroad, and I see no reason why we in the United States shouldn't as a matter of foundational principle do the same. There's no reason diaspora citizens shouldn't have a borderless state of their own that Americans living abroad can affiliate with and express their skills, successes, and suffering thru. Thinking ahead imaginatively if I or someone like me where to be a Precedent someday, I would love to be able to call in or visit the Congressionals from (the State of) diaspora for a conversation about all the best ideas our diaspora noticed worldwide or suggested that they want us to consider for absorption and adoption in our United States, our Lifesaver Labs.

I deeply regret not getting J.'s number and having a way to reach out to him again. It was a nonstop firehose and fountain of wisdom, hard-earned knowledge, and phronesis (practical wisdom) from corners of life I rarely encounter. And honestly, without knowing more deeply about him and his background, my thinking is that someone with a flexible, adaptive, scientific and pragmatic mind like his with the teaching impulse he has could conceivably make a really good Precedent. And if there were a State of Diaspora, I could see people like J. having more of an outsized impact of the vectors the United States takes both at home domestically and in our foreign policy.

November 30, 2025
-----------------
I had a remarkable conversation this afternoon with two brilliant 13 year olds. We talked about a range of subjects, but where they had the most influence over me was in how I think about suffrage process and 13th slave (convict) transitions back into civilian life thru regimented military draft service.

I never got their names, and perhaps that's for the best for the sake of their absolute privacy. I'll refer to them for clarity to keep them separate as A.B. and C.D. 

There's ro (zero) doubt in my mind that these students I spoke with are beyond competent to vote. They probably were psychologically capable and well-informed enuf a year or more ago. One of them, C.D., just had his Bar Mitzvah a few days ago. They're more skilled at public question exploration than a lot of adults I know who don't go into as much free creative depth, or reexplore policy options as freely with fresh eyes. Adults often tend to have more fixed opinions about what's possible in policy; these 13 year olds were perfectly willing to propose new social structures that I don't hear Americans proposing, like 13th slaves (convicts) going into the military for a year or two to transition their way out of prison, learning discipline, rigor, higher self-control, and structure. Russia takes conscripts from the prison population on the promise of faster release to fuel their War in Ukraine, and that's been done many times before to grab more soldiers when countries are desperate for their recruiting goals, but I've never heard of a country systemically sending 13th convicts into military service as part of their final stage of rehabilitation and reintegration. I didn't expect that. While there are details to work out, which they anticipated too, like which types of crime would be cleared for which types of military service, it strikes me as a quite good idea, perhaps Boot Camp for any misdemeanors stretching higher than 9 months. There are also states rights and federalism concerns, but those can be worked out with participating states and federal convicts would be subject to any related federal program independently. 

It reminds me of this TED Talk on [how kindergarten students beat business school grads at building towers](https://www.ted.com/talks/tom_wujec_build_a_tower_build_a_team).

They argued that 14 should be the age to secure the right to suffrage, as they think they'd like to be protected from political divisions and excessive political activisms for one more year until they're out of middle school and into high school. A.B. said he felt like there was a risk students could become overly radicalized and get thrown from their studies by a feeling that their job is to evangelize an inherently flawed candidate or be a partisan for a party. A.B. suggested at the start of the conversation that he thought 16 might be a better age for suffrage guarantee, but the very form of that suggestion was so gently polite and detailed with courtesy that the level of thought his initial deferral suggestion expressed required a level of internal conceptual modeling that demonstrated, beyond any doubt, that he was fit to carry the vote immediately, at 13 certaintly.

We talked about cognitive biases. They felt that students might be biased or ingest bias from their news sources. I told them I was astonished that it took me until late college or after college to find an exhaustive list of cognitive biases that I could dive into and study; I told them I thought it should be commonly taught at a younger age what many of the most common and typical cognitive biases are (like framing error, central tendency bias, and the fundamental attribution error). They also said they could use exposure to a neutral assessment rating of news and media sources across the (civic religious —  my words, not theirs) and political spectrum, knowing which media outlets are considered "right" or "left", "far right" or "far left", "mixed", "independent", or "generally hard news". 

C.B. felt students would almost certainly vote concordant with their parents' votes, expecting 90% to vote that way matching their parents, another 5% to cross over to another part of the spectra, and another 5% to vote for random causes or candidates as a joke, not taking it seriously. I asked him if he thought alike to his parents; he said he did, and that he couldn't think of a single issue offhand that he had a strong disagreement with his parents about. He said they listen to a conservative news channel (Fox News) frequently together and that he mostly agrees with a Fox News view of the world, but he wanted more information on media literacy. He showed great flexibility of thought while we were speaking, but we weren't talking about classically party-ideological issues; I'm not sure what his views might be on more typical political questions and I don't feel a duty to report on them.

One of these students suggested the name "raisingrights" (.org/.us) for the project of attaining suffrage for youth. I like the name a lot; I think it's an excellent suggestion to be combined with the other domain I'm trying to buy. So I bought raisingrights on their suggestion, after questioning whether raisingrites or raisingwrights might be useful too. I decided not to buy those to conserve resources, but they're really great domains too, and I'll probably do the same thing I'm doing on Naybor SOS and cycle thru the homonyms of "Rights" in American English to reinforce the message that we're trying to raise _wrights_ (artful creators) to know, expand, express, and make best use of their _rights_ through the _rites_ of learning and voting. Rights is the most common and simplest spelling, the easiest I agree to build a movement around. I wish I had asked them their opinion on the homophones and homophone cycling. But the more I think about it, the more valuable I think it would be to crosslink raisingwrights.org and raisingrites.org, as ways of letting people share the meaninng they want to emphasize with their parents or friends or calmunity⁵ when they share the concept with others. And an example of a rite on RaisingRites that Lifesaver Labs cares a lot about, aside from fundamental suffrage and mandatory voting which underpins all calmunity⁵ coercions and many public incentives, is universal CPR/AED/First Aid/Overdose training in schools, as part of Ribbreakers United and Naybor SOS. There's a definite tendency to only look to adults to step in in emergencies, but students over the age of 13 have plenty of ability that's only partly developed today to respond to nayborhood crises and [blow us away](https://www.ted.com/talks/adora_svitak_what_adults_can_learn_from_kids).

We also had other conversations like whether we should qualify for meat-eating by killing an animal of that type at some point in our lives over some minmial age of maturity. It came up because they had a fishing pole on them and fishers know what it is to kill a fish and eat the life they take. How many people who eat chicken have slaughtered a chicken? Not me. I eat chicken on Friday night and weekends and I've never had the opportunity to kill a chicken. I eat beef and I've never killed a cow. The nearest thing I've ever done is kill fish while fishing or dissect a fetal pig. They agreed that it seemed reasonable for people to kill at least one of the animal species they eat. One said he loved dogs but he'd be willing to kill a dog if he needed to in extremis in order to eat. I thought that was a very mature perspective though not totally typical of American petowners, who believe that the pets they host at home are a special uber-sacred category, looking down on other peoples who eat dogs with bitter disgust, but don't hesitate to eat chicken (and demand chicken murder) three times a day thoughtlessly, finding the suggestion that they should murder a chicken themselves given the opportunity horrifying and somehow inhumane. There's a lot of strange moral hypocrisy and hair-splitting on animal killing, animal and pet rights and nonviolence, omnivorism, and population management. They asked me if I'm a vegetarian; I told them I'm a weekday vegetarian. I hope it's a pattern that might become more common. And I'd really like to kill a chicken somehow someday so that I can be less of a hypocrite on these beliefs I have that meat-eaters should kill-qualify⁵, conforming to all the compassionate requirements of law like that in the Florida Humane Slaughter Act.

I'm taking time out now this Sunday, having met A.B. and C.D., to work on a first draft of this Raising Rights site while I'm thinking so hard about how much of a fail it is that they can't vote (this year or) by calmpromise next year. I'm working on the prompt and requirements package now. I've got to finish a strong publishable draft tonight; tomorrow I've got to get back to work on Safeword™ and Naybor SOS™ for Lifesaver Labs. But once a draft is out it can evolve and it'll be easier to talk to people about the Raising Rights project and how it connects to other Lifesaver Labs projects.
